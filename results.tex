\section{Results}

\subsection{Preselection}
Performance scores of various estimators elaborated in
Table~\ref{table:AImethods} are presented in Table~\ref{table:regression} for
the regression analyses and presented in Table~\ref{table:classification} for
the classification analyses. The results were obtained using
the default hyper-parameters. Each estimator demonstrated its own characteristic behavior and,
\gls{RF}, \gls{ET}, \gls{KN}, \gls{SV}, and \gls{MLP} estimators predicted the
performance metrics with significantly higher accuracy than the others. Even
without optimizing the hyper-parameters of the estimators, $R^2$ scores of these five estimators
range from 90 to 98\% for regression and from 92 to 96\% for classification.
This implies that the models trained by these estimators will have very low variance.
The \gls{ET} regressor most accurately predicted the
performance metrics while the \gls{SV} classifier was superior to other
estimators in classifying the design labels defined as reactor type, spectrum,
criticality and feedback. 

In general, the linear methods (i.e., SGD,
R, EN, L, LL, P, and Log) yield lower a fit\_score (and thus higher errors) with
respect to the non-linear methods such as ensemble, \gls{SVM}, and \gls{ANN}.
These results clearly imply that the linear methods are not quite enough to
design a nuclear reactor and thus in predicting its performance metrics. Like
the linear methods, the Naive Bayes classification methods such as GNB and BNB
seem inappropriate due to the suboptimal performance scores. In addition to
this, the \gls{RF}, \gls{DT}, B, and AB regressors and classifiers, have similar
results, except for fit score values, as they use the same base estimator
(decision tree) in training. Likewise, the LP classifier produces the same
results with the \gls{SV}  classifier due to the use of the same kernel ($rbf$).
In the regression analysis, the \gls{ET} regressor has the lowest \gls{MAE} and
\gls{MSE} scores while the R regressor has the highest scores. 

As to the
classification, the \gls{SV}  classifier has the lowest \gls{HL} and \gls{LRL}
scores while the Perceptron classifier has the highest scores. Furthermore, any design
can be classified with a small fraction of incorrectly predicted labels ($<$
5\%) and a high precision ($>$ 95\%), leading to an opportunity for an accurate
prediction of reactor design even without making any regression analysis. 

From the results of the preselection phase, five estimators (i.e., \gls{RF},
\gls{ET}, \gls{KN}, \gls{SV},  and \gls{MLP}) satisfying a score of more than
90\% in the $R^2$ and fit score values seem suitable for the subsequent stage
of the calculations. Among the selected estimators, despite its worst fit score
value, the \gls{SV} regressor interestingly shows the second-best performance
right after the \gls{ET} regressor. In fact, all the scores of the five selected
estimators are very close to each other and at this point it is not easy to
distinguish which estimator is best. Therefore, in the following section, the
scores of these five estimators are further improved by tuning their
hyper-parameters.

\subsection{Hyper-parameters optimization and learning curve}
We applied two distinct methods to the selected estimators to improve their performance. 
The first method, expected to have
an impact on the R$^2$ scores, prediction errors and thus cross-validation
results, relies on finding an optimal sample size. As an
illustration, Figure~\ref{fig:lc} demonstrates the learning curves of the
(U-Th)F$_4$-NaF-BeF$_2$ fuel-salt pair for the selected regressors from the
viewpoint of the R$^2$ score and for the selected classifiers from the viewpoint
of multi-label classification accuracy score (f1\_samples). 

From all the learning curves of fuel-salt pairs, random forest (\gls{RF}),
extra-trees (\gls{ET}), multi-layer perceptron (\gls{MLP}), and support vector (\gls{SV}) 
regressors require a sample size in the range
of 32000 and 40000 in which the curves level off to the R$^2$ score of 0.99 and
k-nearest neighbors (\gls{KN}) requires a sample size more than 40000. 
A similar situation was observed in the learning curves of the
classifiers. As a result, we have decided to use about forty thousand samples
for each fuel-salt pair. In other words, for nine fuel-salt pairs, we generated 
around three hundred thousand samples in total. In the second method, we
obtained the optimum
hyper-parameters of the estimators tabulated in Table~\ref{table:hyper}. In the
table, the parameters not listed had optimal default values and required no tuning.

\subsection{Final Estimators}
After performing the hyper-parameter optimization and learning curve assessments, we
calculated the final scores of the selected estimators and listed them in
Table~\ref{table:finalreg} and ~\ref{table:finalclf}. At a quick glance, there
is a slight enhancement in the performance scores of all regressors except for the scores of
\gls{MLP} explicitly exhibiting a strong hyper-parameter dependency. On the other hand, optimization
failed to improve classifier performance.
With the optimized hyper-parameters and sample size in training, the
\gls{ET}, \gls{SV},  and \gls{MLP} regressors now predict performance metrics
with a higher R$^2$ and lower mean absolute error (MAE) compared to the preselection scores.

Likewise, the \gls{SV}  and \gls{MLP} classifiers 
label the design attributes, providing a higher average precision (AP) and
lower label ranking loss (LRL). The \gls{MLP} and \gls{ET} methods demonstrate the lowest errors 
relative to the other estimators and one accordingly promising
candidates for the subsequent phase of this study. To select the right estimator(s) 
for the next phase, the cross-validation plots and multi-label
confusion matrices of each performance metric (i.e., $k_{\infty}$, $\varsigma$,
$\phi_{fast}$) must be investigated.

\subsection{Cross-validation and confusion matrix}
Figure~\ref{fig:cv} visualizes the predicted performance metric values
against their actual values for each estimator. The
cross-validation plots reveal how an estimator
acts on an individual basis of performance metrics within the 5\% relative error
region and show only three (\gls{ET}, \gls{KN}, and \gls{MLP}) of the
estimators accurately predict the performance metrics in accordance with the
acceptance criterion. 

With the given training sample size and hyper-parameters, 
the \gls{ET} is the best option to be used in the
optimization phase. With respect to actual values, it correctly estimated $k_{\infty}$,
slightly underestimated $\varsigma$, and predicted a few values of the
$\phi_{fast}$ metric outside of the region. The \gls{KN} and \gls{MLP} have 
results comparable to the \gls{ET} and
may therefore be considered as alternative estimators for the regression
analysis of channel designs. 

For classification analysis, we presented the
confusion matrices, normalized over the total test sample size, in
Figure~\ref{fig:cm} to compare the predicted labels with the actual labels by
defining the responses in terms of true/false positive and true/false negative
scores. From the prediction capability standpoint, these estimators have very
close scores in each label and predict all labels with a high precision of more
than 95\%. Related to the scores of the labels, the only label that causes the
highest misclassification score by 10\% is the feedback coefficient. This
mislabeling originates from the feedback coefficients very close to zero, which
may not be accurately estimated as they inevitably carry a prediction error. The
other labels are correctly estimated with an error of less than 1\%. As a
result, one of these classifiers can easily handle the classification of channel designs
without notable loss in labeling. In this context, as in the
regression analysis, we decided to use the \gls{ET} classifier in the design
optimization phase of this study to assess the dedicated labels of optimum
designs.

\subsection{Optimum Designs}
In accordance with the constraints applied to the performance metrics,
we found a set of optimum solutions for each
fuel-salt pair. As an illustration, Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} depicts the
pareto-front solutions for the (U-Th)F$_4$-NaF-BeF$_2$, similar to the results
of other fuel-salt pairs. The figure includes (a) the cross-validation plots
for each performance metric, (b) the distribution of the pareto-front solutions
in 3-D performance metric space, and (c) the normalized confusion matrix for the
classification results. From the subplot (a), the pareto-optimal solutions (blue
triangle) overlap very well with the actual values (orange circles) obtained by
the Serpent simulation. Additionally, the solutions show convex distribution in
agreement with the distribution shown in the uppermost subplot of Figure
~\ref{fig:cv}. This situation can be
easily observed from the subplot (b). The other subplots also confirm this
situation by showing good distributions in the very proximity of the
intersection line of each performance metric. 

The cross-validation subplots also
indicate that the predicted metrics totally stay within 5\% relative error
(shaded-area) very close to the intersection line. We found even better results
for the $k_{\infty}$ with no more than 1\% relative error. 

Like the validation
of the solutions obtained by the regression analysis, the classification results
of the optimal solutions in subplot (c), agree very well with the actual
labels. The only noteworthy complication is the misclassification of a
tiny fraction of the feedback indicators. This mislabeling
certainly arises from the prediction of the ${\alpha_{total}}$ values discussed
before and are estimated as positive instead of negative. In conformity with the
constraints applied to the performance metrics in the optimization phase, all
the optimum designs we found are of characteristics of supercritical eigenvalue,
fast spectrum, burner type and negative feedback. 

Of the entire Pareto front solutions (around 650) accounting for each combination 
of the fuel-salt pairs, only the nine
best solutions are tabulated in Table~\ref{table:optimaldesigns}. We simply
opted for the solutions that grant the highest breeding ratio ($\varsigma$) 
and the lowest fast flux ($\phi_{fast}$) at the $k_{\infty}$ = 1.06. The only exception for the
multiplication factor criterion is for the NaCl salt in U-Pu fuel as no
multiplication factor less than 1.21 has been found for this fuel-salt pair.

From the table, the suggested solutions can be grouped more easily by fuel type,
as provided in the table, as the solutions show a certain relationship with the
fuel type rather than salt type. When different fuel types are compared to each
other, the U-Th fuel requires the highest $^{235}$U content (18-20\%) due to the
existence of the $^{232}$Th isotope which has an
absorption cross-section approximately four times higher than $^{238}$U. 
U fuel reaches its top performance when
a moderate amount of $^{235}$U (14-15\%) is used. U-Pu fuel needs the
lowest amount of $^{235}$U (5\%) as not only does Pu in U-Pu already contain a
certain fraction of fissile isotopes like $^{239}$Pu and $^{241}$Pu, but also Pu
fissile isotopes emit greater average fission neutrons per absorption. In a
similar way, we noticed that the U-Th fuel demands about 13-14\% Th in U-Th
while the U-Pu fuel favors the highest grade of U content (90\%). 

A comprehensive look at the common properties of the optimal designs suggests that
$T_{ave}$ should be in the range of 1100-1200 K and $\xi$ needs to be at its
minimum value of 0.274. Furthermore, as expected, Th necessitates higher fissile
material than any other fuel type whereas the fissile material required by the
U-Pu fuel is the lowest as the Pu coming from the weapons-grade already comprises
a great deal of fissile isotopes by almost 50\% of its content. On the other
hand, unlike other feature parameters displaying a direct characteristic
relationship with the fuel type, there is no noticeable evidence to associate
the channel-to-channel pitch length ($p$) with the fuel types but the $p$ is 
likely to vary with the salt type. As
an example, the NaCl calls for a constant pitch value of 1.0 cm in all fuel
types. 

Overall, among the designs, it appears that all the fuels mixed with the
NaCl salt are the most promising designs. In particular, the UF$_4$-PuF$_3$-NaCl
fuel-salt yields the highest $\varsigma$ and $k_{\infty}$. The most unfavorable
side of this fuel-salt is to have a lower ${\alpha_{total}}$ than other salt
types but the coefficient is still quite sufficient compared to the current
designs of \gls{MSR}s at the initial state with a total fuel salt feedback
coefficient of around -3.4 pcm/K \cite{rykhlevskii2019, robertson1971}. From the
reactor safety point of view, with the highest ${\alpha_{total}}$, the
LiF-BeF$_2$ salt is the most favorable.
